人间

The first person she thinks of after his wife is Ah Min. She knows of their friendship, decades long and true.

Her phone feels heavy in her hand as she slides it unlocked, peers down at her screen. Pecks out precious few words.

"I heard."

No response was immediately forthcoming, not that she expected one.

She sets her phone down on the table, takes a sip of Perrier.

Picks up her phone again.

"I'm here," is all she types again, a quiet invitation, a silent opening of her door, if Ah Min needed or wanted. An offering of self, freely given.

It wasn't until a week later that she received any reply.

"Are you in Beijing?"

She wasn't, not at that particular moment, but she could be, tomorrow. She turns and looks meaningfully at her assistant, a darling young woman that was a study in empathetic efficiency.

"Yes?"

"Can you get me a ticket on the earliest train to Beijing tomorrow morning?"

Her assistant's brow furrows for a few seconds. She tips her phone and shows her Ah Min's message and all confusion clears.

"I should be able to make the arrangements. I'll also push the meetings for the next few days back, or arrange for them to be by video call."

"Thank you. I don't know what I'd do without you."

"It's nothing."

Her assistant turns to hurry away, but suddenly pauses. Turns to look her boss in the eye.

"Take care of her. We all like her too."

Startled, she nods. Tries to ignore the implication that her assistant and her entire team had included her in the "we" that liked Ah Min.

(Because who was she to deny that?)

She focuses instead on replying Ah Min, reassures her that she will be in Beijing the next day and that she would send across her travel details as soon as she could.

"You didn't need to come specially."

"I know."

She smiles a small smile, can already sense the mix of relief and petulant propriety in Ah Min's response. Decides the tiniest bit of levity would be appropriate.

"So... if i didn't need to come specially, should I cancel my train ticket? I don't think they've booked it yet."

"Just send me what time you expect to arrive at Beijing South."

"Alright."

The ticket secured for her was blessedly a business class seat, albeit on a punishingly early 5.17 am train. She packed light, stuffing everything she needed for a three day trip into a small carry on case. Mask on and hair in a messy bun, she boards her train with seconds to spare, nods gratefully to the cabin staff that helped her find her seat, put her case away.

The journey is just over two hours, and she takes the time to complete her morning's homework, blitzes her way through the sutras and mantras that had become her daily bread for the past 20 years. Breakfast was a simple bun and a coffee from the trolley. She keeps the complimentary goodie bag, contemplates the various meat-based snacks as she tucks them into her oversized satchel.

"Well, that's the least I could bring her, I suppose," she mutters, turns to gaze out the window at the rising sun. Now midautumn had passed, the weather had begun its slow descent into winter. The autumn sunrise is still beautiful, and she loses herself in the blur of the countryside whipping past her window, lit amber and red.

"Next stop. Beijing South," comes the automated announcement, genteel and mild.

She blinks and gathers herself, checks that she hadn't left anything behind as she reaches for her case. She gets an odd glance or two from the only other passenger in her compartment, thankfully empty apart from the both of them. She smiles behind her mask and nods when they make eye contact, confirming without engagement her identity.

A bitter wind whips through the train platform as she steps off and she tugs her scarf closer around her neck. Beijing is drier and cooler than she expected, and she hurries to the nearest escalator, eager to get indoors.

It is only as she steps off the escalator that she realises that she hadn't planned further than getting to the train station, nor did Ah Min offer an address to meet at. Deciding to deal with the problem after she'd cleared security, she strides with purpose toward the train station concourse.

She's fishing her phone out from her pocket mid-walk towards some empty seats on the concourse when she feels a warm hand land on hers, wrestling the grip of her suitcase away from her. Her instinct is to pull away, when she catches whiff of a familiar perfume.

"Ah Min!?"

"Yes."

"Why are you here?"

"Picking you up from the station."

"It's 7 am."

"I was already awake."

She takes a closer look, charitably keeps to herself the thought that it wasn't that Ah Min was already awake, it was more probably because Ah Min never slept.

"Have you had breakfast? Let's go get a cup of coffee first?"

"I've already eaten."

"Well I haven't," she lies, for both of their sakes, and wrests control of her case back from Ah Min, stomps with purpose towards the Starbucks she had spotted.

She orders herself a tooth achingly sweet caramel latte, lets Ah Min order herself a plain black coffee with a shake of her head. Decides to split a blueberry muffin between the two of them, a marginally healthier choice of sweet treat from the case by the counter.

Their coffees are drunk mostly in silence, and Ah Min only eats when the plate with half a muffin is nudged towards her. Grief sits heavy on Ah Min's shoulders, a stark contrast to when they had last seen each other, at her concert in Shanghai. She lets the silence lay between them, a heavy thing thrumming with shared emotion.

"You ready?"

She nods, brushes the crumbs from her scarf.

This time, Ah Min simply takes her case and she lets her, follows her lead out of the coffeeshop. She walks next to Ah Min, lets her knuckles brush against the back of Ah Min's hand. Smiles a small smile behind her mask when she feels a reciprocal nudge, the telltale sign of how under it all, Ah Min still couldn't hide her need for physical closeness.

Ah Min's car is a reasonably understated electric SUV, and she climbs in the front seat, settles her tote between her feet.

The vehicle comes to life with a quiet chime, and the sound of her own voice, singing Sarah McLachlan's Angel fills the cabin, unexpectedly loud.

In the car

Angel · Chyi Yu
Over the Cloud

"Sorry," Ah Min mutters, as she swipes at the control on her steering wheel, lowers the volume.

"That's ok," she replies, decides not to comment on the choice of music. In her heart of hearts, she understands.

(Knows without asking Ah Min's desire for some light, to pierce grief's veil. That was the emotion she had put into recording that track, and she knows now Ah Min's very being had heard so, had sought some solace there.)

The journey back to Ah Min's home is shorter than she anticipated, especially given the morning rush-hour traffic. They sit mostly in silence as a carousel of her songs from (insert album names here) play. Midway through The Wayfaring Stranger Ah Min reaches for her and she goes willingly, lets their joined hands rest on Ah Min's thigh as they wait at a red light. The muscle flexes beneath the back of her hand and she laces her fingers with Ah Min's, silent support as her voice from 15 years ago plays about them. They are both thinking about him, she realises. A wayfaring stranger no more was he, gone to rest.

At the red light

The Wayfaring Stranger · Chyi Yu
Over the Cloud

The light turns green, and Ah Min untangles her fingers, grips the steering wheel in a death grip. They inch forward.

Eventually, the car rolls to a stop in the driveway of a large private property and she nods thoughtfully, takes in the immaculately kept garden. Ah Min hauls her case from the boot and moves towards the front door, looks expectantly at her.

“You have a lovely garden,” she says, and Ah Min’s lips quirk slightly in acknowledgment, a polite smile that doesn’t quite reach her eyes.

They step across the threshold at the same time and she unashamedly takes a moment to look around as she shoves her feet into borrowed house slippers, hums a tuneless murmur of appreciation at the late morning light streaming in through the many large windows. Ah Min places her keys in a bowl on the sideboard with a clatter and props herself against the wall, unabashedly watches her guest inspect her home, folds her arms as she stares, hollowly.

Chyi Yu turns, is about to pay Ah Min yet another polite compliment on the design of her home when she catches sight of Ah Min, feels in an instant the weight of her emotion in that raw gaze, even from a distance. The words die on her tongue and she crosses the room in two quick strides, bundles Ah Min into a hug unasked instead.

She feels the surprised gasp against her ear and then she’s staggering forward as Ah Min wraps her arms around her, pulls her into herself.

“I’m here now,” she murmurs, runs a soothing hand down Ah Min’s back.

Ah Min squeezes tighter and she goes willingly, perches on tiptoes to hold her all the better.

Distantly, she’s aware of the sound of birdsong and wind, a quiet thread through an open window. Her right shoulder is damp, and she feels the shuddering breaths taken, emotion and grief that could no longer be contained.

All too soon, the ache in the balls of her feet becomes pressing and it is with no small regret that she pulls back, applies gentle force so she is once more fully on solid ground.

Ah Min refuses to let her go fully, laces her fingers in the small of her back, leans down to press their foreheads together, uncaring about her tearstained face as the silent tears continue.

She reaches for her cheeks in an echo of the last time they stood on a stage together, presses cool palms to warm tears, wipes them away with deft fingers. Utters the greeting she should have when she first saw Ah Min, were it not for the public about them.

“Hello, pretty girl,” she croons, and Ah Min’s eyes flutter open. She sees all at once the unfiltered exhaustion and grief staring back at her and her heart breaks anew. A blink, and then there is a flash of something else as Ah Min’s eyes flicker downwards and back up to meet hers.

Desire, she realises, and is surprised at how unsurprised she is.

(after all, they were both of them only human, no matter how much they denied themselves)

Somewhere outside, a crow caws, and she pulls back, gently takes Ah Min’s hands in hers.

“Let’s sit down,” she suggests, strokes her thumbs over Ah Min’s knuckles.

A sniffle, and then a nod and Ah Min turns, continues to hold her left hand in her right. She pauses to scoop up her tote with her free hand, nods with a smile to signify her readiness.

Ah Min leads her to the living room, the sharp lines of the house’s architecture softened by rugs and throws in neutral colours.

“Something to drink?”

Ah Min’s voice is low and hoarse.

“Warm water, please and thank you.”

Her host nods and disappears around the corner. She sits forward in the sofa, rummages in her bag for the meat-based snacks from the train. Lines them up neatly in a row on the low coffee table, peers curiously at the books stacked under the glass surface.

A book on mid-century architecture in North America, a coffee table book on the history of Chinese fashion, another on Europe’s opera houses.

“Here,” Ah Min reappears as she is contemplating the books, sets down two insulated mugs on coasters.

“Thank you. Those are for you, by the way,” she grins, nods at the snacks.

Ah Min snorts a laugh at that.

“Who told you I hoard these?”

“I figured it was the least I could bring you, I didn’t have time to buy you a proper present. Besides, I don’t like wasting food, and I won’t eat them.”

“Thank you then,” Ah Min quips, in Shanghainese, and she chuckles.

“Most welcome, Ah Min meimei.”

Ah Min’s head snaps up at that and a blush suffuses her cheeks. She clears her throat.

“Are you hungry?”

“Not yet, but if we order food now, I should be by the time the food gets here. Do you have any favourites nearby?” She reaches for her mobile phone.

Ah Min clears her throat again.

“I was going to cook for us,” she says, voice uncharacteristically small. She does not meet her gaze.

“I don’t want to trouble you. You must be tired.”

“It’s nothing. I’ve already made the vegetarian dumplings.”

“Oh? When did you make them?”

Ah Min doesn’t meet her gaze, scuffs a foot against the shagpile carpet as she mutters “last night” under her breath.

She resists the urge to sigh and instead congratulates herself on her powers of observation. So she had been right about Ah Min’s lack of sleep.

“Well then, if you insist on spoiling me, how could I possibly say no?”

Ah Min all but leaps to her feet at that, sudden manic energy at being of service.

“I just need twenty minutes!” she quips, strides away with purpose, leaving her quite alone in the living room.

A clock on a far wall ticks through the silence and she takes a contemplative sip of water. Sleep, she decided, was of utmost priority for Ah Min. She casts a sideways glance at the sofa she is still sitting on, gives it an experimental bounce or two and decides that it would do for her after-lunch nap plans. She gets to her feet with a small grunt and cradles her mug in her hands, follows the faint sounds of a meal being cooked around the corner.

Ah Min’s back is to her, motions practiced and smooth as she slides ingredients into a pot, reaches into a cupboard for a pair of matching bowls. The kitchen is smaller than she expected. Ah Min is humming something under her breath, something familiar but unnamed, a melody that reminds her of some form of home.

She clears her throat.

Ah Min whirls around and nearly drops the bowls.

“Careful - I was just going to ask you where the restroom was, if I could freshen up.”

“Second door down that corridor.”

“Thanks. That smells good, by the way.”

She is waved away with a self-deprecating scoff, and she is gladdened to see a secret smile and a blush on Ah Min’s cheeks.

The guest bathroom is the same flavour of magazine ready as the entryway and living room of the house were. She takes her time returning to the kitchen, stops to look at the framed pieces of art hanging on the walls, peers in curiosity at the labels beneath each of them, gallery style.

Only two names are listed across the three paintings she looks at, and she realises with growing fondness that these were works of Ah Min’s children.

“I like the art you’ve got hanging on the walls,” she quips, as she re-enters the kitchen.

Ah Min laughs at that, a burst of brightness bouncing about kitchen tile.

“They’re by my kids,” she says.

“I know - your daughter has a particularly good grasp of the use of the colour purple.”

An inelegant snort.

“That was a phase she went through when she was eight,” she snarks, even as she continues with preparing lunch, tips some soup into a waiting bowl

“You must be very proud of them,” she says, and means it, sincere and solemn.

“I am. They remind me of their father a lot, sometimes.”

She does not quite know what to say to that, the presence of another dead man’s memory bloomed to life in the silence between them.

Ah Min’s smile is almost apologetic as she places their lunch on a tray, two bowls of matching dry noodles and dumplings, two bowls of clear soup.

“Come on, let’s eat,” she says, picks up the tray and leads the way out of the kitchen, away from the dull memory of what she had lost.

They dine at a table by the french doors that open to the courtyard in the middle of the house. She does not force conversation, asks instead about the (frankly excellent) food.

“You must teach me one day how to make these dumplings. Do you have a recipe?”

“Oh - it’s just something I threw together. I’m not too sure about the exact proportions…”

She puts down her chopsticks with a clink and stares. Decides flattery was the best distraction from grief.

“That’s not fair.”

“What?”

“It’s not fair that you’ve got so much talent - you can sing, you can cook, you’ve clearly got great taste in interior design. And so beautiful and tall, too.”

“I - “

She grins, sits back in satisfaction at the blush suffusing Ah Min’s cheeks, racing down her neck.

Ah Min huffs at her self-satisfied smirk.

“I know what you’re doing.”

“Well, is it working?”

“Be quiet and eat your noodles.”

The tips of Ah Min’s ears remain pink, and she obediently finishes the food, takes the bowls to the kitchen sink despite a tirade of protests. Washes them as Ah Min swaps their mugs of water for floral tea, a begrudging compromise of her hostly duties.

“There. All done. Don’t you dare do them again,” she warns, as she catches Ah Min looking with longing consternation at the drying dishes, wipes her hands on a nearby kitchen towel.

“Living room,” she commands, takes Ah Min by the elbow and steers her out the kitchen.

Unceremoniously she flops down onto the sofa, looks up expectantly at Ah Min who furrows her brows at her. She sets their mugs back down on their respective coasters and sits down primly, noticeably further away than she had, during the livestreams.

She raises an eyebrow, looks Ah Min square in the eye and taps the side of her right thigh, wordless encouragement for her to come closer. Her invitation is taken swiftly and Ah Min leans close, left hand pinching and smoothing the knees of her jeans as she fights the urge to simply take.

“I have never stopped you,” she murmurs, and thinks to herself - nor will I ever. Ah Min takes the permission for what it is, grabs her hand in a vice grip, slouches down to lay her head on her shoulder. She laces their fingers together, squeezes gently to convey her reassurance.

“Thank you for coming,” Ah Min says into their silence, after a while.

She blinks awake, realises with a start that she had herself nodded off when she had meant for Ah Min to rest.

“Of course,” she says, simply. Untangles their fingers in favour of putting an arm around Ah Min, offers her left hand across her lap instead. The offer is taken and Ah Min curls even closer, tucks her face against her neck. She fights a shiver when the warmth of a sigh ghosts across her skin, wills herself to focus on the music she suddenly notices has started playing softly in the background instead.

“What're you playing?”

“A French playlist, given to me by Huan’er.”

She stills. Listens closer. A woman sings a tale of silver melancholy and her heart aches with lightness and grief in equal measure. Ah Chloris.

From Huan's playlist

À Chloris · Reynaldo Hahn · Susan Graham
Reynaldo Hahn · La Belle Époque

“It's lovely,” she offers, as the track ends.

“I made a joke once about wanting to become more cultured and the next week he sent this to me, with handwritten notes explaining why and how he felt about each piece.”

She smiles.

“Music was always the most important thing to him,” she murmurs, thinks back to 2019.

I should have kept more in touch, she thinks. Glances down at the treasure in her embrace, decides to do better.

“This was his favourite, he said,” Ah Min murmurs, as the next piece begins.

“What is it about?” She asks, into Ah Min’s crown, lifts a hand to thread fingers through her hair. Absently she seeks out the tightness at the base of Ah Min’s skull, applies pressure experimentally. A gasp of relief slides warm and moist against her neck and she forces herself to focus again, digs deep into the knowledge of traditional Chinese medicine she’d taken to learning, all those years ago.

She works her way upwards, scratches gently at Ah Min’s scalp and is rewarded by a rumbled hum of pleasure and a shuffle closer, Ah Min’s nose pressed against her skin.

“S’about a rose,” comes a sleepy mumble that she feels more than hears.

“Oh?” Her fingers do not cease their movement, only slow to match the cadence of Ah Min’s voice.

“A dream by a girl, that the rose she wore to her first ball was speaking to her… the translation is quite beautiful and bittersweet.”

A yawn, stifled against her throat.

“Shh. Sleep. Tell me about the roses and the girl and Huan’er later.”

The fight goes out of Ah Min then, and soon she is a heavy dead weight against her. She watches the light change through the windows, hears the distant whisper of an evening breeze. She dozes too, drifts in and out of dreams she is glad she does not remember, and when she wakes again it’s to purpling darkness out the windows, a stiff neck and a rumble of hunger in her belly.

Still, Ah Min sleeps.

Experimentally, she tugs her left hand free from Ah Min’s sleep loosened hold, places Ah Min’s hand on her knee instead. Reaches for her mobile phone so she can at least start looking at getting dinner delivered for them.

She sighs when she realises that she doesn’t have the exact address for delivery. Nor does she have her glasses.

“Ah Min,” she tries, nudges with her right shoulder gently.

“...”

“……”

“Ah Min,” she says again, moves her right hand to tug gently at Ah Min’s right earlobe.

That draws a response, a heavy hand swatting at her fingers.

She chuckles and repeats the motion, takes the chance as Ah Min’s head finally lifts from her shoulder to say, directly in her left ear, voice pitched low: “Ah Min.”

A gasp, and then an unexpectedly bony elbow to the ribs as Ah Min suddenly scrambles away, backs up against the arm of the sofa, knees drawn up to her chin.

“Ow,” she says, pointedly, rubs at her side.

“I”m sorry -”

The apology is wide eyed and she shakes her head with a rueful smile.

“Don't be. Come here. Let's order dinner.”

She pats the sofa cushion next to her and Ah Min shuffles cautiously closer, reaches a little helplessly for where she'd elbowed her. Hesitates, fingers hovering an inch from fabric.

Mournful, liquid eyes meet hers and she cannot help but laugh.

“I'm really alright, Ah Min.”

A pout.

“Nothing a little rubbing and ointment won't sort out, later. Now, dinner.”
